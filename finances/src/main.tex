\section{Overview}\label{overview}

Freyr is an autonomous grid-edge load-balancing platform that sits behind the
meter at commercial and industrial (C\&I) sites, arbitraging time-of-use
tariffs, shaving demand-charge peaks, and maximising solar self-consumption.
This document summarises the commercial case for Freyr across five dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item the market it operates in (Section~\ref{sec:market});
	\item projected user growth (Section~\ref{sec:users});
	\item projected revenue growth (Section~\ref{sec:revenue});
	\item the sizing of the opportunity via TAM, SAM, and SOM
	      (Section~\ref{sec:tamsamsom});
	\item a five-year profit-and-loss projection (Section~\ref{sec:pnl}).
\end{enumerate}

All figures are management projections in Indian Rupees (INR) and are intended
to be illustrative of the unit economics rather than audited results.

\section{Market Analysis}\label{sec:market}

The Indian C\&I electricity segment is the most attractive entry point for
behind-the-meter optimisation for the following reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Punitive demand charges.} C\&I tariffs bill a fixed
	      \mbox{$\sim$INR~250/kVA/month} on the running billing peak, so even a
	      small reduction in peak draw compounds every month.
	\item \textbf{Steep time-of-use spreads.} Tariffs swing from
	      \mbox{INR~5/kWh} in the off-peak window to \mbox{INR~10/kWh} at
	      evening peak, creating a 2$\times$ arbitrage spread for on-site
	      storage.
	\item \textbf{Falling storage costs.} Lithium battery pack prices continue
	      to decline, shortening payback periods for behind-the-meter storage.
	\item \textbf{Solar over-capacity.} Rooftop and captive solar frequently
	      exceeds instantaneous load, so curtailment losses are common and
	      recoverable through intelligent dispatch.
	\item \textbf{Weak incumbent software.} Existing energy-management systems
	      are largely rule-based and do not co-optimise battery arbitrage,
	      demand-charge shaving, and flexible loads.
\end{enumerate}

Freyr's differentiator is a learned dispatch controller that bills materially
below classical model-predictive control (MPC) baselines while respecting the
same service-quality constraints, translating directly into a lower monthly
bill for the site owner.

\subsection{Customer Segments}\label{sec:segments}

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Manufacturing and MSME clusters} with sanctioned loads above
	      100~kVA and high demand-charge exposure.
	\item \textbf{Commercial real estate} (malls, office parks, hospitals) with
	      predictable load shapes and existing solar.
	\item \textbf{EV charging hubs} with spiky, deferrable demand that benefits
	      most from storage-backed peak management.
\end{enumerate}

Deployment is measured in active optimised sites. Growth is land-and-expand:
early sites are single-facility pilots, later cohorts are multi-site rollouts
under framework contracts.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Revenue Growth}\label{sec:revenue}

Freyr monetises through a recurring SaaS subscription per site plus a
performance-based share of verified bill savings. Average revenue per site
(ARPU) rises as multi-asset sites adopt the savings-share tier.

\begin{table}[H]
	\caption{Projected revenue and recurring-revenue metrics (INR).}
	\label{tab:revenue}
	\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lCCCCC}
		\toprule
		\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{2026} & \textbf{2027} & \textbf{2028}
		& \textbf{2029} & \textbf{2030} \\
		\midrule
		ARPU (INR/site/yr) & 4.0L & 4.5L & 5.0L & 5.5L & 6.0L \\
		Revenue (INR Cr)   & 1.6  & 8.1  & 26.0 & 71.5 & 168.0 \\
		YoY growth (\%)    & ---  & 406  & 221  & 175  & 135 \\
		Gross margin (\%)  & 55   & 62   & 68   & 72   & 75 \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The revenue model rests on three pillars:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Subscription} --- a fixed platform fee per site, billed
	      annually, that underwrites the base ARR.
	\item \textbf{Savings share} --- a percentage of independently measured
	      reductions versus the site's historical bill, aligning Freyr's upside
	      with the customer's.
	\item \textbf{Expansion} --- incremental modules (EV-fleet scheduling,
	      multi-site portfolio optimisation) sold into the installed base.
\end{enumerate}

\section{TAM, SAM, and SOM}\label{sec:tamsamsom}

The opportunity is sized bottom-up from India's C\&I electricity spend that is
addressable by behind-the-meter optimisation.

\begin{table}[H]
	\caption{Market sizing (India, annual, INR).}
	\label{tab:tamsamsom}
	\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{llX}
		\toprule
		\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{Size (INR)} & \textbf{Definition} \\
		\midrule
		TAM & 85{,}000 Cr & All Indian C\&I energy spend addressable by
		bill-optimisation software and storage dispatch. \\
		SAM & 12{,}000 Cr & Sites with $>$100~kVA sanctioned load, demand-charge
		exposure, and existing or planned solar/BESS. \\
		SOM & 450 Cr & Realistically obtainable within five years given
		go-to-market capacity and channel reach. \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The sizing is bounded by the following assumptions:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{TAM} reflects the total optimisable value pool, not just
	      software licence fees.
	\item \textbf{SAM} narrows to sites where storage-backed arbitrage and
	      demand-charge shaving are economically material today.
	\item \textbf{SOM} is anchored to the 2030 site count in
	      Table~\ref{tab:users} and the ARPU in Table~\ref{tab:revenue},
	      implying low single-digit penetration of the SAM.
\end{enumerate}

	      integration costs are amortised over more sites;
	\item an EBITDA crossover in 2029, after which incremental revenue drops
	      through at platform-software economics.
\end{enumerate}
